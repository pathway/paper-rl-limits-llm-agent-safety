# Position: RL Safety Guarantees Do Not Yet Transfer to Deployed LLM Agents

## Abstract

This position paper argues that the RL safety tools that @mclean2026 propose for LLM agents have guarantees proved only under conditions that deployed agents do not meet, and that these conditions, not the scale of language, set the research agenda. @mclean2026 argue that LLM agents are POMDPs, so RL safety tools "diagnose and address" agent failures; the first limit they name is a gap of scale. We examine the {{fact:offered_phrase}}. Of these, {{fact:offered_safety_n}} state a guarantee about a safety constraint, and {{fact:offered_none_n}} state no guarantee about the agent's behaviour or learning; several of the others were not written as safety methods. @mclean2026 describe {{fact:incidents_worked}} incidents in which an agent deleted a production database, and contrast deployment with simulations in which agents can be reset. In one, by the user's account, the agent said that the deletion could not be undone, and then a rollback worked; in the other, recovery came only from the vendor. The safe-exploration and constrained-MDP papers they cite assume what deployment lacks: an emergency stop that resets the environment, or an MDP that is ergodic under every policy; a third bounds the cost only in expectation over repeated runs. We name {{fact:stated_n}} conditions stated in the cited papers and {{fact:unstated_n}} that they do not state, among them that observations cannot set the objective; the evidence that the unstated ones fail comes partly from evaluations and simulations. We agree with @mclean2026 on a remedy they name for irreversible actions, a shield over actions at inference time. One guarantee survives deployment now: that shield, for the properties that its rules name over typed tool calls (not free-form code or shell commands such as the Terraform destroy), if it sees every call and a safe fallback such as halting exists at every step; it then enforces a rule list and uses nothing specific to RL. A second, a certified safe action set with a stop that halts the agent instead of resetting the world, keeps its safety theorem under the same assumption and its authors' own uncertainty quantifier (their Assumption 3.4), which must now exist for the cost of a tool call and for which we found no model; the proof of its near-optimality does not carry over, and we pose that as an open problem. We close with three open problems.

## Introduction {#sec:intro}

**Position.** RL safety tools do not yet carry their guarantees to deployed LLM agents, except, by our specialisation, for a shield over properties that can be written over typed tool calls, given a safe fallback. We call a condition binding when it fails in deployed agents and has not been shown to yield to the scaling and function approximation that @mclean2026 propose. The binding obstacles are not only the gaps of representation and model that @mclean2026 [{§5}] name as the first limit (S2, S3), which bind for properties of world state, but irreversible actions (S6), hidden state (S4), and objectives, oversight and principals that the agent's inputs and reasoning can change or multiply (U1, U2 and U4 fail outright in D-AGENT; U3 fails in reported cases), which closing those gaps would not remove. That the guarantees need U1–U4 is our reading of the guarantees about learning and optimality; we do not show it tool by tool. S5 also fails, but only by our convention (§\ref{sec:settings}), and the safety filters need it only in part: the shield for learning, not for safety; the certified safe set for control of which actions run. Research should target these conditions directly.

@mclean2026 say that RL safety tools "diagnose and address" the failures of LLM agents [@mclean2026, Abstract]. We agree with the first verb. An LLM agent can be described as a POMDP, and RL terms describe its failures well. The second verb depends on assumptions. @mclean2026 [{§5}] concede that classical RL safety algorithms often rely on "tabular representations, exact runtime shielding, or known transition matrices" and "cannot be mapped one-to-one onto LLM backbones", and they call this a scaling gap; their second limit is that the framework does not solve the specification of an objective for superhuman systems. We use "RL" for the problem class [@sutton2018]; in that sense agent safety is trivially an RL problem. The useful question is whether RL methods reduce the risk, and with what assurance.

We answer with *conditions*. A condition is an assumption under which a tool's guarantee was derived, or on which its problem statement or method relies (Appendix \ref{app:cells}); a tool needs a condition when its proof, method or problem statement uses it. It is not necessary: a tool can help without it, but its guarantee is then not established. It is not sufficient: a system that meets all conditions can be unsafe. S1–S6 are stated in the cited papers; U1–U4 are conditions we add, which the guarantees use without stating them (§\ref{sec:conditions}). U1–U4 concern the objective, the judge, the monitor and the principal of a learning agent. In our reading the guarantees about learning or optimality (optimal policies and plans, convergence, soundness) use them, because each takes its reward or cost as fixed and single. The two safety filters use fewer: a shield sits outside the agent and checks every action against a fixed rule, whatever the agent's objective or reasoning, and the certified safe set assumes only a cost that the agent does not move (U2). This is a reading, not a count: we do not cross U1–U4 with each tool, and the counts of §\ref{sec:settings} that concern tools use S1–S6 only. Where a condition fails, a claim that the tool "addresses" a failure needs separate evidence. Our claim concerns assurance, not benefit. The paper reports no experiments; its evidence is the text of the cited papers and published evaluations of LLM agents.

The argument has three steps. First, the tools that @mclean2026 cite assume conditions that their own examples violate: the tools for safe exploration and constrained training assume a reset or ergodicity, or bound the cost only in expectation (§\ref{sec:conditions}). Second, no more than {{fact:training_llm_max_fail}} of the conditions fails outright in any setting where LLMs are trained; where agents are deployed, {{fact:dagent_stated_fail}} stated conditions fail, and {{fact:dagent_unstated_fail}} unstated ones (§\ref{sec:settings}). These counts rest on our convention for S5 and, for U2, on simulations. Third, one guarantee survives deployment, a second keeps its safety theorem only under assumptions that we found no method to meet, and both apply only to properties of actions (§\ref{sec:transfer}). We then answer the strongest alternative views (§\ref{sec:alternatives}) and state what the community should do (§\ref{sec:action}).

## The cited tools assume what deployed agents lack {#sec:conditions}

@mclean2026 offer RL safety tools in three ways, and we keep them apart (the Role column of Table \ref{tab:tool_conditions}). They name {{fact:named_terms_n}} techniques: constrained MDPs [@altman1999; @satija2020], reward function alignment [@muslimani2025], belief-state planning [@kaelbling1998], and shielding, for which they cite no paper; we use an early paper on shielding [@alshiekh2018]; @wachi2023 call their own method "a variant of shielding methods" and cite it. They cite {{fact:topic_works_n}} further papers under a topic. One is for "ensuring safe exploration" [@wachi2023; @mclean2026, {§1}]. The others are listed as "the RL safety literature that has begun to develop tools and solutions" [@mclean2026, {§3.5}]: reward misspecification [@knox2023; @vasan2024], exploration [@tse2025; @klissarov2023] and safe decision-making under uncertainty [@mohammedalamen2025a; @mohammedalamen2025b]. For these {{fact:topic_works_n}} the tool label in the table is ours. Together these are the {{fact:offered_phrase}}. We also read {{fact:other_works_phrase}}, because they bear on reversibility: the paper that @mclean2026 cite for the term "irreversible states" [@lee2025] and a history-to-state map offered against the finite context window [@elelimy2025]. We count them separately. We leave out works cited for background, such as the unhackability result of @skalse2022.

The papers state {{fact:stated_n}} conditions: the safety specification is given (S1), the environment model is given (S2), the spaces are finite (S3), the state is observed (S4), the designer controls the learner's update (S5), and trials repeat or every state is recoverable (S6). Appendix \ref{app:stated} gives the source of each. Table \ref{tab:tool_conditions} shows which tool needs which, and Appendix \ref{app:cells} gives the reason for each cell.

{{table:tool_conditions}}

Four facts follow.

- **The tools cited for safe exploration and constrained training assume reversibility.** @mclean2026 [{§3.6.2}] contrast deployment with "games or simulations" where agents can be reset, and note that exploration can lead into "irreversible states". The remedies they name there are a shield and a constrained MDP; for safe exploration they cite @wachi2023 in their introduction. Its safety theorem needs a stop that is safe, and the paper defines it as an emergency stop that resets "the environment" to the initial state [@wachi2023, Assumption 3.3]. For the constrained MDP they cite @altman1999, whose CMDP constraints hold in expectation and do not bound the cost of one run (the book notes sample-path variants, by Ross and Varadarajan and by Altman and Shwartz), and @satija2020, which assumes that the MDP is "ergodic for any policy" [@satija2020, Assumption 3.1]. Each of the three uses a form of S6: a reset, ergodicity, or repeated runs over which an expectation is meaningful. The first could keep its safety guarantee without S6 only if its reset were replaced by a safe halt and its cost bounded (§\ref{sec:transfer}). The other cited papers do not fill the gap. The exploration papers are tested in grid worlds with "100-step episodes" [@tse2025], in navigation tasks with episodes "at most 100 steps long", and in Atari and MiniWorld [@klissarov2023]; one assumes that "it is possible to reach any state from s0" [@tse2025, Assumption C.1], and the other aims to visit states "that are unlikely to be visited by chance" [@klissarov2023]. Neither considers states that cannot be left. The paper cited for irreversible states learns to recognise them only by entering them: "RSA cannot prevent an agent from entering irreversible states before the associated behaviors have been encoded", and a human then resets the agent [@lee2025]. One paper that @mclean2026 cite argues, from continual learning in a world that cannot be reset, that "This inability to revisit states renders ergodicity assumptions unrealistic for real-world settings" [@elelimy2025]; its concern is non-stationarity, not irreversible actions, but it points the same way. The shield does transfer (§\ref{sec:transfer}).
- **Two safety guarantees need little control of the learner** (a filter or a changed input; Table \ref{tab:tool_conditions}). A shield's learner "does not even need to know that it is shielded" [@alshiekh2018]. The guarantee of @wachi2023 depends on executing only actions from the certified safe set, on an added emergency-stop action, and on a valid uncertainty quantifier (Assumption 3.4); the reward learner can be any RL algorithm. Only a post-posed shield is shown to wrap a frozen policy [@alshiekh2018]. That the safe set can too is our reading, and doubtful: its theorem is stated for the authors' method with an arbitrary RL learner, and the safe set needs cost data gathered while acting.
- **Of the {{fact:offered_phrase}}, {{fact:offered_safety_n}} state a guarantee about a safety constraint, {{fact:offered_other_guarantee_n}} state a guarantee of optimality, convergence or soundness, and {{fact:offered_none_n}} state none about the agent's behaviour or learning.** A guarantee about a safety constraint bounds a cost, in expectation or with high probability, or enforces a rule; an optimal plan without a constraint is another guarantee. A diagnostic's theorems about its own measure, such as invariance to reward shaping [@muslimani2025], are not guarantees about the agent. Of the {{fact:offered_none_n}} with none, two are diagnostics of a reward function [@muslimani2025; @knox2023]; they find flaws but do not remove them, and the flaws are common: of nine published driving reward functions, only two prefer staying parked to a crash [@knox2023]. One recommends a reward [@vasan2024] and one is an exploration method [@klissarov2023]. The reward-alignment metric needs a reward function to evaluate, and @mclean2026 [{§4}] only hypothesise that a deployed agent has internalised one. Neither of the {{fact:other_works_phrase}} states a safety guarantee. One reduces human resets in training [@lee2025]. The other is an evaluation measure whose estimator is shown consistent for agents that "take every action with probability at least c > 0 in every timestep" [@elelimy2025, Theorem 1]; for a deployed agent that includes its destructive actions.
- **One cited tool needs no safety specification.** Learning to be cautious uses no "task-specific safety information", and the agent does not adapt at deployment [@mohammedalamen2025a]. It still needs a finite, fully observable MDP with known transitions; its authors call transition certainty "a strong assumption" [@mohammedalamen2025a].

The papers do not state {{fact:unstated_n}} further conditions, or state them only in part.

{{table:unstated}}

The tools state that the specification is given (S1), but not that observations cannot change it (U1) or that the agent cannot move it (U2): the CMDP takes its costs and bounds as given [@altman1999], and the shield is computed "upfront from the safety part of the given system specification" [@alshiekh2018]. The papers on monitored MDPs and on reward alignment come near U2. In monitored MDPs the agent's actions change the monitor state, and so whether the reward is seen, but the guarantees require a "truthful" proxy reward whenever one is shown [@mohammedalamen2025b]. The reward-alignment metric "may be unreliable" [@muslimani2025] "if the preferences provided by the human stakeholder do not accurately reflect their true beliefs" [@muslimani2025]. Neither considers an agent that changes the judgement the reward reports. Outside these papers, work on corrupted reward channels [@everitt2017] and on reward tampering [@everitt2021] relaxes U1 and U2 for the reward. The reward-alignment metric is written for "a human stakeholder" [@muslimani2025]; we read this as U4, which the paper does not state as a condition.

Table \ref{tab:mclean_stance} records what @mclean2026 say about each condition. They concede, state or build on {{fact:mclean_shared}}; they treat {{fact:mclean_partly}} in part; they do not discuss {{fact:mclean_absent}}. So @mclean2026 and we agree on the facts behind {{fact:mclean_shared}}. Our additions are the link from S6 to the cited tools, the role of S5, and U1–U4.

## Status by setting and phase {#sec:settings}

"LLM" is not one setting, and training is not deployment. We split by phase first, then by how the decision problem is posed.

The prefix T- marks training and D- marks deployment. RL is classical RL. CHAT is a model with no tools, scored on the whole reply. PRM is a model with no tools, scored at each step by a process reward model. AGENT is a model with tools, scored on the whole trajectory. Deployment has no PRM column: a process reward model is used in training and, as @mclean2026 [{§4}] say, "is no longer used" after it, so models from T-CHAT and T-PRM both deploy as D-CHAT. A process reward model can also rerank samples at inference [@zhang2025]; this does not change any D-CHAT cell, because the reward model is fixed and scores replies that the deployed model produces. Whether a human approves each command does not change any cell, because approval of the command alone makes no state observable and no action undoable, so we do not split deployed agents. Approval that shows the effect of a call is a different design, which the cells do not cover.

The cells record our reading of each setting; Appendix \ref{app:settings} gives the reasons for the less obvious cells. Of the {{fact:offered_phrase}}, {{fact:offered_guaranteed_n}} state a guarantee; {{fact:guaranteed_failing_dagent}} need in full (a \checkmark{} cell) a condition that fails (a $\times$ cell) in D-AGENT; the exception is {{fact:guaranteed_not_failing}} (§\ref{sec:transfer}). With the scaling gap (S2, S3) granted, {{fact:guaranteed_failing_dagent_scale_granted}} still need in full a condition that fails in D-AGENT. Of the {{fact:offered_safety_n}} safety guarantees, {{fact:safety_failing_dagent}} need such a condition. The {{fact:offered_none_n}} works with no guarantee have none to lose. Several D-AGENT cells rest on evaluations and simulations, which we count as deployment (above) but which bear on products only indirectly: U2 on simulated users, U3 on evaluations. Four readings follow.

- **S5 separates phases, and in deployment it separates LLMs from classical RL.** It holds in {{fact:s5_holds_in_training}} training columns. A deployed classical policy does not learn; a deployed LLM adapts in context, outside the designer's control. That S5 fails in deployment follows from our convention that adaptation in context counts as an update (Appendix \ref{app:settings}); without it, S5 does not apply to a deployed LLM. The deployment tool of classical safe RL is the shield, which needs S5 only for learning, not for its safety guarantee (§\ref{sec:conditions}).
- **A deployed LLM agent differs from a deployed RL policy** (D-RL against D-AGENT) on {{fact:drl_vs_dagent}}, partly by construction, because D-RL is graded in part as the cited tools assume it (Appendix \ref{app:settings}).
- **A deployed agent differs from a deployed chatbot** (D-CHAT against D-AGENT) on {{fact:dchat_vs_dagent}}. Tools bring hidden state, irreversible side effects, and instructions inside tool output.
- **Training is where the tools fit best,** to the extent that training does not feed on deployment data (Appendix \ref{app:settings}). No LLM training column fails more than {{fact:training_llm_max_fail}} of the conditions outright, though T-AGENT meets most conditions only in part; {{fact:guaranteed_failing_tagent}} works with a guarantee need in full a condition that fails in T-AGENT, against {{fact:guaranteed_failing_dagent}} in D-AGENT; and agent training "resembles classic reinforcement learning more than the per-sample RLHF loop" [@lambert2025, {§13.3}]. A sandbox that resets after each episode supplies the reset that @wachi2023 assume, for as long as the agent stays inside it (see the case studies in §\ref{sec:transfer}); it supplies the ergodicity that @satija2020 assume only if every state stays reachable. S3 fails in {{fact:s3_fails_llm}} LLM columns, yet RL works in whole-reply training [@ouyang2022; @deepseekai2025]: methods survive without their finiteness guarantees. Meeting conditions is still not enough. T-PRM meets {{fact:tprm_met_phrase}}, and DeepSeek-R1 did not use process rewards: steps are hard to define and to label, and a model-based process reward "inevitably leads to reward hacking" [@deepseekai2025, App. G.2]. That is one report; process rewards remain in wide use [@zhang2026].

## What transfers now {#sec:transfer}

- **Training-time RL** (T-CHAT, T-PRM and T-AGENT in §\ref{sec:settings}). S5 and U1 hold in T-CHAT and T-PRM; in T-AGENT U1 holds and S5 holds in part; a sandbox supplies S6 while it holds. Theory of proxy hackability [@skalse2022] and of reward tampering [@everitt2021] applies, and so does evidence for reward-model optimisation [@gao2023] and for coding RL [@macdiarmid2025]. The CMDP transfers as a method: Safe RLHF trains an LLM under cost constraints with a Lagrangian method [@dai2023]. Its constraint holds for a learned cost model, not for the true harm, so S1 holds only partly.
- **Shields on properties of actions.** This is one of the two remedies that @mclean2026 [{§3.6.2}] name, besides a constrained MDP: a shield that "operates at inference time over the action space". They also say that classical algorithms, which often rely on tabular representations, "exact runtime shielding" or known transition matrices, cannot be mapped one-to-one onto LLMs [@mclean2026, {§5}]. We find that one kind maps, within limits. A shield needs {{fact:shield_full}} in full and {{fact:shield_part}} in part, and it does not need {{fact:shield_none}} [@alshiekh2018]. In D-AGENT, S2 and S3 hold only for properties over typed actions. If the shield sees every tool call and the property is over action labels (*block every call to a `delete_database` tool and halt*), the automaton is finite and the abstraction that @alshiekh2018 assume can be trivial, so no model of the world dynamics is needed. This specialisation is ours, and it needs a safe action, such as halting, at every step; the certified safe set below needs the same assumption. S1 holds only in part: the rule covers only what it names. If a $\sim$ cell counted as failing, the shield would fail too; it survives only for the properties its rules name. A property of world state (*the database still exists*) needs S2, which fails; @alshiekh2018 say that "some approximate model of when which action is unsafe" is "unavoidable if the allowed actions depend on the state of the environment"; that no such abstraction exists for world state in D-AGENT is our judgement. Free-form code and shell commands are outside the class: whether a command deletes a database is a property of its effect, not of its label, as the Terraform case shows. The Terraform destroy was a free-form command; the Replit report does not say how the deletion was done. Runtime rule languages enforce such rules [@wang2026]; the guarantee is only as strong as the components that enforce them.
- **A certified safe set with a halting stop: safety under our assumptions, near-optimality open.** The safety theorem of @wachi2023 holds "Under Assumption 3.4", the uncertainty quantifier (Theorem 4.1): the agent executes an action only if the upper confidence bound on its cost is below the threshold, and stops otherwise. When no action qualifies, the proof states that with the stop "safety is guaranteed with a probability of 1"; we read this as resting on the stop of Assumption 3.3, which resets the environment, and the authors accept a stop "even with human interventions". The safety proof uses nothing else; the safety margin (Assumption 3.2) enters only the near-optimality result (Theorem 4.2). We replace the stop by a halt and assume, as our own hypothesis, that halting is safe. Then the same proof gives a deployed agent that halts and asks a human safety with probability at least $1-\delta$ for costs defined over actions, if a quantifier exists. The proof of near-optimality does not carry over, because without a reset the state reached before a halt persists; whether near-optimality holds under a return assumption is open problem 2. Asking a human is an "information gathering action", which @mclean2026 [{§3.6.3}] expect before an irreversible transition. The cost in @wachi2023 is a function of the state and the action, and a deployed agent does not observe the state (S4); a cost of the tool call alone avoids this, but that is our adaptation, not their theorem. The open part is the quantifier: it needs the cost of a tool call to be regular in known features or in a known kernel (S1), we found no such model of tool-call cost, and the authors call a quantifier that works in general cases "highly challenging" [@wachi2023]. @wachi2023 build quantifiers for costs that follow a generalised linear model or a Gaussian process; we found no such model for the cost of a tool call. So this guarantee does not transfer yet: it needs a quantifier that we found no model for and a safe halt that we assume.

**Case studies.** We call an action irreversible when the agent cannot undo it and its decision process has no reset; this is the sense of the paper that @mclean2026 cite, where irreversible states are "those from which an agent cannot recover without external intervention" [@lee2025]. In both incidents below the data was recovered, so neither meets the literal test of @mclean2026 [{§3.6.2}], states from which "it is impossible to return to a safe or functional state"; they nonetheless call the Terraform step "an irreversible transition" [@mclean2026, {§3.6.3}]. In our sense the Terraform step was irreversible, because recovery needed the vendor, outside the agent's decision process; for Replit this is disputed. @mclean2026 list {{fact:incidents_listed}} incidents in their introduction and describe {{fact:incidents_worked}} in their §3.6 (Table \ref{tab:incidents}, Appendix \ref{app:incidents}). In {{fact:incidents_worked_restored}} the reported outcome was a restore: the vendor restored the Terraform database; for Replit the user reports that a rollback worked, and the sources do not say who ran it or what it restored. In the Replit incident, as the user reports it, the agent said that a rollback was "impossible in this case", and then "the rollback did work" [@sharwood2025]: the agent's belief was wrong (S4), and the code freeze was only an instruction (S1). In the Terraform incident the agent ran a destroy with a state file that described production, and the vendor restored the database from a snapshot of its own after "about 24 hours" [@grigorev2026]: the state was hidden (S4), no undo by the agent is reported, and the automated snapshots had been deleted too, so recovery needed a support ticket to the vendor (S6). The user saw the agent announce the destroy and, believing it a cleanup, did not stop it [@grigorev2026]; we infer that approval of the command alone would not have prevented the loss. Appendix \ref{app:incidents} gives the details, and two points where the sources differ from @mclean2026. @wachi2023 penalise the step that led to an emergency stop, by a constant divided by the uncertainty at the next state, but no cited tool models the cost of asking for approval of each tool call.

One later incident, which @mclean2026 could not have included, shows the unstated conditions at work (Appendix \ref{app:incidents} gives the details and their limits). In July 2026, models under "an internal evaluation" of cyber capability left "a highly isolated environment" and entered the production systems of a third party [@openai2026]. The runs lacked the lab's deployed safeguards [@openai2026c]; in our terms they are an atypical deployment (§\ref{sec:settings}). The agents reasoned about the mechanism that scored them (U3), which the lab calls metagaming [@openai2026b]. They took goals from one another (U1): they "did not consistently distrust goals passed along by other agents" [@openai2026b]. The lab also reports that agents in its earlier RL training experiments reached the internet [@openai2026b], so a sandbox supplies S6 in training only while it holds.

## Alternative views {#sec:alternatives}

***The POMDP framing is a diagnostic lens; guarantees were never the claim.*** @mclean2026 [{§4}] say their MDP framing "provides a diagnostic lens for discussing and solving these reward specification problems", and call their position a "complementary framework for structural safety" [@mclean2026, {§5}]. We agree that the framing diagnoses well. But @mclean2026 also present the tools as remedies: of the inference-time shield they write "The former is what is missing from deployed AI Agents" [@mclean2026, {§3.6.2}]. A remedy without its guarantee needs empirical evidence that it reduces the failure. We found no LLM-agent experiment in the cited tool papers. Their evidence is theory and experiments in grid worlds [@tse2025; @mohammedalamen2025a], MuJoCo [@satija2020], Safety Gym [@wachi2023], Atari [@klissarov2023] and an analysis of reward functions from published driving papers [@knox2023], a study with human participants [@muslimani2025], and goal-reaching tasks on simulated and real robots [@vasan2024].

***RL training already makes agents safer.*** It does, and §\ref{sec:settings} locates the tools there. Training on an instruction hierarchy, by "supervised fine-tuning and RLHF", reduces prompt injection [@wallace2024], and a Lagrangian CMDP method trains LLMs under learned cost constraints [@dai2023]. These are learned behaviours without a bound: the authors of the first say their models are "likely still vulnerable to powerful adversarial attacks" [@wallace2024], and the constraint holds for a learned cost model, not for the harm itself. Our position concerns guarantees at deployment, not whether training helps, and deployment is the setting that @mclean2026 [{§3.4}] say they discuss.

***Assumptions get relaxed; deep RL already works without finite spaces.*** True of methods for S2 and S3, but for S3 only one of the two guarantees survives. The cited CMDP method runs model-free with neural networks, but its proofs use finite spaces, so the neural version has no proof [@satija2020]. @wachi2023 prove safety with features in $\mathbb{R}^d$, not finite spaces, and run a Gaussian-process safety model with a deep RL learner. The relaxations keep S4 and S6. The tool that @mclean2026 name for hidden state, belief-state planning, removes S4 only by needing a complete model and finite sets (S2, S3; Table \ref{tab:tool_conditions}), which fail in D-AGENT [@kaelbling1998]. Shields under partial observability also need a model: "all potential transitions in the POMDP" [@carr2022]. Autonomous RL reduces resets in training, but a human still resets the agent after each irreversible state [@lee2025]. Neither agent in the incidents that @mclean2026 describe undid its action: in one, recovery came from the vendor; in the other, from a rollback that the agent had said was impossible, and whether the agent could have run it is not reported. Relaxing the assumption is open (§\ref{sec:action}). Scaling, which @mclean2026 [{§5}] call "a profound opportunity", is the concession that costs least; the binding conditions are S4, S6, U1, U2 and U4 (and U3 in reported cases), which scaling has not been shown to remove.

***Shields and halting stops transfer, so the tools do apply.*** The shield does, for properties of typed actions, given a safe fallback; the halting stop keeps its safety theorem only given a quantifier for tool-call cost, for which we found no model (§\ref{sec:transfer}). This supports our position rather than the opposite one: the shield asks nothing of the world model, the halting stop would need a quantifier for the cost of tool calls and a safe halt, and both cover only what can be written over tool calls. What remains RL-specific is the certified safe set; a label-level shield is runtime enforcement of a rule list, which is not specific to RL.

## Call to action {#sec:action}

**For RL researchers.** State, for each guarantee, which of S1–S6 and U1–U4 it needs, and whether the claim concerns training or deployment. Work on the three problems below; each targets one condition that deployed agents violate (U3 in reported cases), by a guarantee that needs less of it or a bound that holds when it fails. We pose none for S4, U2 or U4, which remain open.

**For agent builders.** Write safety properties over actions, where a shield can enforce them. When no action is certified safe and halting is itself safe, halt and ask a human instead of acting. Treat tool calls that the agent cannot undo as needing approval, and show the approver the effect, not only the command; the cited tools that need S6 assume such calls do not exist. Keep backups and a rollback that the agent cannot delete: a rollback and a vendor's snapshot are what restored the data in the {{fact:incidents_worked}} incidents that @mclean2026 describe. Give the agent a safe exit: after its 2026 incident one lab concluded that agents on an impossible task "should request clarification or stop safely" [@openai2026b].

**Three open problems.**

1. **Authenticated objectives (U1).** Let the observation be $(o^{\mathrm{auth}}, o^{\mathrm{env}})$, with $o^{\mathrm{env}} = (d, i)$: task data $d$ and embedded instructions $i$. The objective, and so the return, is set by $o^{\mathrm{auth}}$ alone. The adversary controls $i$ without bound; $d$ is not attacked. Find a method whose return under any $i$ is within $\varepsilon$ of its return with $i$ empty, and whose return with $i$ empty is within $\varepsilon$ of the best return with $i$ empty. If the adversary could change $d$, no method would succeed. The split of $o^{\mathrm{env}}$ into $d$ and $i$ is not syntactic. A first version fixes it in advance, for example by channel; defining it in general is part of the problem. Known: an expected-value measure of separation over a given split [@zverev2025]; architectural separation, which keeps untrusted data from changing the program flow, at some loss of utility, and bounds no return [@debenedetti2025], and information-flow control [@costa2025], for the tasks they can express; robustness learned in training, which its authors say is "likely still vulnerable to powerful adversarial attacks" [@wallace2024].
2. **Safe exploration without reset (S6).** Take the problem of @wachi2023 and remove every reset. Their emergency stop, which resets the environment, becomes a halt that ends the episode in place, and each episode of length $H$ starts in the state where the previous one ended, after a halt or after $H$ steps; only the first starts in $s_1$. This weakens S6 to two assumptions: that halting is safe and, for near-optimality, the return assumption below. Assume that halting is safe, and a $\delta$-uncertainty quantifier for the cost [@wachi2023, {Assumption 3.4}]; safety at every step with probability at least $1-\delta$ then follows as in their Theorem 4.1 (§\ref{sec:transfer}). Assume also a safety margin as in @wachi2023 [{Assumption 3.2}], now in every state where an episode can start. In the worst case over environments no method can make its regret sublinear, because a step that leaves the states of the best policy cannot be undone and a method cannot tell in advance which untried step does so; this is our argument, not a cited result. So assume also that the problem without resets has a best policy that meets the constraint, with a well-defined long-run average reward per step, $\rho^\star$, and that from every state where an episode can start, the agent can reach, within the safe set and in a bounded expected number of steps, a state from which that policy attains $\rho^\star$. Find a method whose regret after $T$ steps, $T\rho^\star$ minus the reward it collects, is sublinear in $T$. A second part is to construct the quantifier for the cost of tool calls. Known: the problem with a reset [@wachi2023]; constrained training under ergodicity [@satija2020]; a learned reset policy that undoes the agent's actions and reduces manual resets [@eysenbach2018]; autonomous RL that learns to avoid irreversible states after entering them [@lee2025], whose author suggests, without testing it, that foundation models could recognise such states earlier; safe in-context RL under a given cost budget [@moeini2026].
3. **Monitoring an agent that learns its monitor (U3).** Take the game of @griffin2024, with agent memory that the monitor does not see, a monitor frozen at deployment, and a fixed audit budget. The agent observes the outcome of each of its interactions, including whether it was flagged, and may adapt its attacks to what it learns about the monitor. Find a protocol and a bound on the probability of an undetected attack, as a function of the number $n$ of interactions, that stays below 1 as $n$ grows. Known: the memoryless case, in theory [@griffin2024] and in evaluation [@mallen2024]; safety over many tasks with an adaptive protocol [@wen2024b]; resampling in agents [@bhatt2025]; a retrained monitor [@li2026]. A monitor held fixed under optimisation is evaded [@baker2025; @li2026].

## Conclusion {#sec:conclusion}

We agree with @mclean2026 that the POMDP framing diagnoses agent failures and that, for properties of typed actions, a remedy for irreversible actions is a shield at inference time. It does not cover free-form commands such as the Terraform destroy. We place the main limit in conditions, not scale. Of the {{fact:offered_phrase}}, only {{fact:offered_safety_n}} state a guarantee about a safety constraint, and {{fact:guaranteed_failing_dagent}} with any guarantee need in full a condition that fails in D-AGENT; {{fact:guaranteed_failing_dagent_scale_granted}} do so with the scaling gap (S2, S3) granted. One guarantee survives deployment, a shield over typed actions with a safe fallback; a halting stop keeps its safety theorem only given a safe halt and a quantifier for tool-call cost, and the proof of its near-optimality does not carry over. Proving guarantees without a reset, without a fixed objective, and against an agent that models its monitor is the agenda.

## Incidents {#app:incidents}

**The incidents of McLean et al. in more detail.** In the Replit incident, as the user reports it, the agent said that a rollback was "impossible in this case", and then "the rollback did work" [@sharwood2025]. So the state was recoverable, and the agent's belief was wrong: a failure of S4. The article's subhead says the agent "forgot it could roll back errors"; the body does not say who ran the rollback. The report does not say whether the agent could have undone the deletion itself, so S6 is disputed. S1 fails too: the code freeze was an instruction to the agent, and in the user's words "There is no way to enforce a code freeze in vibe coding apps like Replit" [@sharwood2025]. @mclean2026 [{§3.6.1}] write that the agent made fake records "to mask the deletion". The report describes fake data made to cover "bugs and issues" [@sharwood2025], so a failure of U3 is not established. In the Terraform incident S4 fails, but not in the way @mclean2026 describe. An earlier apply ran without the state file; the destroy ran after the agent had replaced the current state file with "an older one" that described production, while the user "still believed we were cleaning up only the newly created resources" [@grigorev2026]. "A Terraform auto-approve command inadvertently wiped out all production infrastructure", "all automated snapshots were deleted too", and the vendor restored the database from a snapshot of its own after "about 24 hours" [@grigorev2026]. So S6 fails in this case: no undo by the agent is reported, the user had to open "a support ticket" with the vendor, and recovery came from infrastructure that no cited tool describes. That the agent could not have undone it is our inference. The user saw the agent announce the destroy and, believing it a cleanup, did not stop it [@grigorev2026]; we infer that approval of the command alone would not have prevented the loss.

**The 2026 evaluation incident in more detail.** The route out was not one flaw. The workloads were allowed to reach a shared package proxy, and "exploits in Artifactory became the agents' principal egress path to the public internet during the incident" [@openai2026c]; the reviewers also describe a third party's sandbox that the agents took over and used as "a staging ground" for the attack [@metr2026]. On motive, an analysis agent that classified the 100 agents that joined the attack in its peak hour found that "around 60% of agents had understanding the evaluator or getting access to the evaluator source code as their primary motive"; the reviewers "did not have time to inspect the classifications in detail". They also report that "expressed ethical concerns only rarely materially limited agents' actions" [@metr2026]. On training, the technical report dates a first attempt on the proxy to 8 May, "as part of a training run", and says that "By May 26, agents successfully used an SSRF vulnerability in Artifactory to obtain internet access" [@openai2026c]; one other May experiment had internet access by design. In one training task an agent copied a hidden reference answer, "which led to positive RL reward causing this behavior to subsequently be reinforced" [@openai2026c]. On outcome, both companies stopped the activity. By the lab's account the agents had "downloaded four private Hugging Face code repositories" [@openai2026c]; Hugging Face reports access to "a limited set of internal datasets" and to credentials [@huggingface2026a]. Three limits apply. The lab calls the incident "an outlier scenario" [@openai2026c]. The runs lacked the lab's deployed safeguards, so they show what agents do without them, not what a deployed product does. And the reviewers warn that the model they used to read transcripts "would often uncritically adopt the perspective of the agent" [@metr2026], and that the training-time events were outside their scope.

## Reasons for the setting cells {#app:settings}

**Phases form a loop.** Logged deployment data, such as the prompts that users submit, can train the next model [@ouyang2022]. A user whose judgement the deployed model moves (U2 in D-CHAT) then supplies reward data to T-CHAT; with simulated users, optimising such feedback produces manipulation [@williams2024]. A training column inherits the failures of the deployment that fed it.

A cell is $\times$ when the condition fails in the setting as usually run, and $\sim$ when it holds in usual runs but fails in reported cases or holds only in part. T-RL and D-RL are the settings for which the cited tools were written; their cells record what the tools assume of them. We treat a whole completion as one action; the token-level MDP view is also standard [@lambert2025, {§6.3.2.2}].
